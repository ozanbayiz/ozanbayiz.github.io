> **Abstract.** We evaluate two 4B-parameter vision-language models (VLMs)
> on grade-school math in five languages, independently varying the language
> of the question, its modality (text or rendered image), and the language the
> model reasons in. Cross-lingual accuracy is compositional: a two-term linear
> model in the model's monolingual accuracy on source-language images and its
> monolingual accuracy on target-language text predicts held-out cross-lingual
> accuracy with LOOCV $R^2 = 0.875$. Reading the question from the image
> accounts for most of the error; the choice of reasoning language and prompt
> strategy accounts for little.

## 1. Introduction

Small VLMs run on-device, where cloud models are expensive or unavailable. A
student who photographs a worksheet and asks for help poses a task with two
steps: the model reads the question, which may be in any language and may
arrive as pixels, and then reasons to an answer, possibly in another language.
Multilingual benchmarks report one accuracy for the whole task, so when a
model fails, the score cannot say whether it misread the question or
reasoned badly.

We measure the two steps separately. We take the 250 MGSM test questions in
five languages, present each as plain text and as a rendered image, and ask
the model to reason in each of the five languages in turn. Every combination
of question language, modality, and reasoning language is its own condition,
so a model's monolingual scores (reading and reasoning in the same language)
can be compared against its cross-lingual scores (reading in one language,
reasoning in another).

<mark>Cross-lingual visual reasoning in small VLMs decomposes into reading the
source language from pixels and reasoning in the target language as text, and
the model's monolingual scores on each step predict its cross-lingual
accuracy.</mark> Three further findings follow from the same grid: reading
limits accuracy far more than reasoning does, English is a robust reasoning
language for non-English inputs, and asking the model to translate first does
not help.

## 2. Setup

**Models.** Gemma 4 E4B and Qwen3.5 4B, 4-bit quantized, run locally with MLX
on Apple Silicon. Inference only, greedy decoding.

**Data.** The 250-question MGSM test set in English, German, Spanish, Chinese,
and Turkish. MGSM has no Turkish split, so we translated the full test set; we
release the translation with the evaluation code.

**Modalities.** Each question appears as a text string and as a PNG rendered
at 300 DPI (Fig. 1).

**Prompt strategies.** Cross-lingual conditions use three prompts.
**Original** asks the model to reason in the target language and leaves the
transfer implicit. **Translate** asks it to translate the question first, then
solve it. **No-Translate** asks it to solve the question directly and forbids
translation.

**Notation.** A cell is one model, one data language $d$ (the language of the
question), one reasoning language $c$, one modality, and one prompt strategy.
$A_t(d, c)$ is accuracy on text input and $A_v(d, c)$ is accuracy on image
input. Cells with $d = c$ are monolingual; cells with $d \neq c$ are
cross-lingual, and we call them off-diagonal. Five languages give 20
off-diagonal pairs, so each modality has 120 off-diagonal cells across both
models and all three strategies (20 pairs × 2 models × 3 strategies).

The full grid is 5 data languages × 5 reasoning languages × 2 models × 3
strategies × 2 modalities: 300 conditions and 75,000 question-level
evaluations.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Reading limits accuracy

Average accuracy over all language pairs:

| Modality | Gemma 4 E4B | Qwen3.5 4B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Text | 86.1% | 83.3% |
| Image | 73.5% | 78.3% |

Image input costs 12.6 points on Gemma and 5.0 on Qwen, and the data language
drives most of that loss. On Gemma, English images average 86.8% and Chinese
images 53.2%, a 33.6-point gap. For a fixed data language, changing the
reasoning language moves accuracy by at most 14 points. The bottleneck is
getting the question out of the pixels.

### 3.2 Monolingual scores predict cross-lingual accuracy

For each off-diagonal cell we ask whether its accuracy can be predicted from
the model's monolingual scores alone. We fit a linear model with two
predictors, one for the source side and one for the target side, and score
the fit by leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV). For text input, the
predictors are the model's monolingual text accuracy in the data language and
in the reasoning language:

$$
\hat{A}_t(d, c) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\, A_t(d, d) + \beta_2\, A_t(c, c),
\qquad R^2 = 0.693.
$$

For image input, the source-side predictor becomes monolingual image accuracy
and the target-side predictor stays text:

$$
\hat{A}_v(d, c) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\, A_v(d, d) + \beta_2\, A_t(c, c),
\qquad R^2 = 0.875.
$$

This model fits all 120 off-diagonal image cells (Fig. 2). Replacing the
target-side predictor with monolingual image accuracy $A_v(c, c)$ lowers the
fit. Once the model has read the question off the image, reasoning in the
target language is a text task.

### 3.3 English is a robust reasoning language

For non-English inputs, English reasoning is often the best or tied-best
choice, and the tradeoff is asymmetric. Where native-language reasoning
wins, it wins by at most 2.8 points; where English wins, the margin reaches
9.2 points (Gemma on Chinese images). A deployment restricted to one
reasoning language should use a high-resource one.

### 3.4 Explicit translation does not help

Translate-then-solve lowers image accuracy on Gemma from 73.5% to 71.3% and
has inconsistent effects on text. Leaving the transfer implicit is the
simplest effective choice. Across the grid, prompt strategy moves accuracy by
2 to 3 points, an order of magnitude less than the data language does.
Prompting cannot supply a capability the model lacks.

## 4. Robustness

Gemma's Chinese-image cells form a low outlying cluster (Fig. 2), and a
regression across two clusters can report a high $R^2$ while explaining
little within either. Two checks address this. Adding per-language and
per-model group controls raises the target-reasoning coefficient $\beta_2$
from 0.61 to 0.67. Removing every Gemma × Chinese cell leaves both predictors
highly significant, with $R^2 = 0.79$. The compositional structure survives
without its worst cluster.

## 5. Limitations

We study two models, both 4B-parameter, 4-bit quantized, and decoded greedily;
larger or full-precision models may compose differently. The task is
grade-school math, where reading and reasoning separate cleanly, over five
languages and 250 questions per language.

## 6. Conclusion

In small VLMs, reading the source language from pixels and reasoning in the
target language as text are separable capabilities, and training coverage in
one does not substitute for the other. A model that solves Turkish math
problems from worksheet photos needs Turkish visual-language data and strong
text reasoning in some language. Prompt strategy closes neither gap.

**Data and code.** The Turkish translation of the MGSM test set and all
evaluation code are released with the paper.
