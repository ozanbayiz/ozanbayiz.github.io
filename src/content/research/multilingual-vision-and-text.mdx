## Overview

Small vision-language models can run on-device, which makes capable AI
available where cloud models are expensive or unavailable. But to be useful, a
small VLM has to do two things in sequence: read a question, possibly written
in one language and possibly rendered as an image, and then reason toward an
answer, possibly in a different language. Most multilingual benchmarks
collapse those two steps into a single accuracy number, so when a model fails
you can't tell which step failed.

We separate them. We evaluate two 4B-parameter VLMs (Gemma 4 E4B and Qwen3.5
4B) on grade-school math from MGSM in five languages, independently varying
three things: the input language, the input modality (plain text vs. a
rendered PNG), and the language the model reasons in. Since standard MGSM
doesn't include a lower-resource language like Turkish, we translated the full
250-question test set and release it along with all evaluation code.

The headline: cross-lingual performance is compositional. It decomposes into
"read the problem" plus "reason about it," and the two pieces are independent
enough that a two-term linear model predicts held-out cross-lingual accuracy
remarkably well.

## Setup

Both models run locally, 4-bit quantized, via MLX on Apple Silicon, inference
only with greedy decoding. Each of the 250 questions appears in two
modalities: as a plain text string, and as a PNG rendered at 300 DPI.
Cross-lingual cells get three prompt strategies: **Original** (reason in the
target language, transfer left implicit), **Translate** (explicitly translate
first, then solve), and **No-Translate** (solve directly, translation
forbidden).

The full grid is 5 data languages × 5 reasoning languages × 2 models × 3
strategies × 2 modalities: 300 conditions and 75,000 question-level
evaluations.

## What we found

**Vision is much harder than text, and the gap is about reading, not
reasoning.** Text accuracy averages 86.1% (Gemma) and 83.3% (Qwen) across all
language pairs. Vision drops to 73.5% and 78.3%. The spread across *data*
languages in the vision setting is enormous: English images average 86.8% on
Gemma while Chinese images average 53.2%, a gap of over 30 points. Changing
the *reasoning* language for a fixed data language moves accuracy by at most
14 points. The bottleneck is getting the question out of the pixels.

**Cross-lingual accuracy is predictable from monolingual scores.** For each
cross-lingual cell we ask: can you predict it from the model's
native-language accuracies alone? For text inputs, source-language text
ability plus target-language text ability explains the data (LOOCV R² =
0.693). For image inputs, the source-side predictor shifts to vision:
source-language *visual* ability plus target-language *text* ability gives
LOOCV R² = 0.875 over all 120 off-diagonal cells. Swapping in target-language
vision ability instead makes the model worse, which is the interesting part:
once the problem has been read off the image, reasoning in the target language
is a text operation. The model doesn't need to see anymore.

We were suspicious of that 0.875 ourselves, since Gemma's Chinese-image
cluster sits far below everything else and a line through two clusters can
look great while saying little. Two checks: adding per-language and per-model
group controls *strengthens* the target-reasoning coefficient (0.61 → 0.67),
and deleting every Gemma×Chinese cell outright keeps both predictors highly
significant with R² = 0.79. The compositional structure isn't an artifact of
the worst cluster.

**Reasoning in English is a robust default.** For non-English inputs, English
reasoning is frequently the best or tied-best choice. The tradeoff is
asymmetric: where native-language reasoning wins, it wins by at most 2.8
points; where English wins, it can win by up to 9.2 points (Gemma on Chinese
images). If you're deploying a small VLM and have to pick one reasoning
language, pick a high-resource one.

**Forcing translation doesn't help.** The explicit translate-then-solve
pipeline is mildly harmful in the vision setting (Gemma: 73.5% → 71.3%) and
inconsistent for text. Leaving the transfer strategy implicit is the simplest
effective choice. Overall, prompt strategy moves accuracy by 2 to 3 points, an
order of magnitude less than the data language does. Prompting can't paper
over missing capability.

## Takeaway

For small VLMs, multilingual visual reasoning is two independent capabilities,
not one: reading the source language from pixels, and reasoning in the target
language as text. Training coverage in one channel does not substitute for the
other. If you want a model to solve Turkish math problems photographed from a
worksheet, it needs Turkish visual-language data *and* strong text reasoning
somewhere, and no prompting trick closes either gap.
